A. THEORETICAL QUESTIONS (3 marks each)

Q6.1: Interference Condition [3 marks]

What are coherent sources? State the conditions for constructive and destructive interference. Why are ordinary light sources incoherent?

* Concept: Coherent sources maintain a constant phase difference and same frequency, allowing stable interference patterns. Interference occurs when waves superpose, leading to constructive (bright) or destructive (dark) regions based on path difference.

Formula: * Constructive Interference (Bright Fringes): Path difference Δx = mλ (m = 0, 1, 2...) * Destructive Interference (Dark Fringes): Path difference Δx = (m + ½)λ (m = 0, 1, 2...) * Intensity: I = Imax cos²(δ/2) where δ is phase difference.

Solution: Coherent Sources: Sources are coherent if they maintain a constant phase difference and have the same frequency. Waves from coherent sources can produce stable interference patterns.

Conditions for Interference: 1. Coherent Sources: Essential for a stable pattern. 2. Monochromatic Light: Single wavelength. 3. Small Source Size: To ensure point sources. 4. Constant Phase Difference: Maintained over time.

Why Ordinary Light is Incoherent: Ordinary light sources (like incandescent bulbs) emit light from individual atoms that radiate independently and for very short durations (~10⁻⁸ seconds). The phase of the emitted light changes randomly and rapidly. Therefore, two separate ordinary light sources cannot maintain a constant phase relationship, making them incoherent. This random phase variation prevents the formation of a stable, observable interference pattern. Lasers, on the other hand, produce coherent light through stimulated emission.

Q6.2: Diffraction vs Interference [3 marks]

Distinguish between interference and diffraction. Why does diffraction occur? Can an object have both interference and diffraction simultaneously?

* Concept: Interference is the superposition of waves from two (or more) separate coherent sources. Diffraction is the bending of waves around obstacles or through apertures, where a single wavefront is modified.

Formula: * Interference (Double Slit Bright): d sinθ = mλ * Diffraction (Single Slit Minima): a sinθ = mλ (These formulas describe the positions of maxima/minima in their respective patterns.)

Solution: Comparison:

| Feature | Interference | Diffraction | |---------|--------------|------------| | Origin | Superposition of waves from two or more coherent sources | Bending of waves from a single wavefront around an obstacle/aperture | | Pattern | Well-defined, equally spaced bright and dark fringes (if sources are identical) | Central maximum is brightest and widest; subsequent maxima are progressively dimmer and narrower | | Conditions | Requires coherent sources, usually from division of wavefront or amplitude | Occurs with any wave when it encounters an obstacle/aperture of comparable size to its wavelength | | Dependence | Depends on distance between sources (d) | Depends on size of obstacle/aperture (a) |

Why Diffraction Occurs: Diffraction occurs because, according to Huygens' Principle, every point on a wavefront acts as a source of secondary wavelets. When a wavefront encounters an obstacle or an aperture, the wavelets originating from the unblocked portions of the wavefront spread out into the region behind the obstacle or aperture, causing the wave to bend around corners and spread into areas that would otherwise be geometric shadows. This bending is more pronounced when the wavelength of the wave is comparable to the size of the obstacle or aperture.

Can Both Occur Simultaneously? Yes, absolutely. In many practical situations, interference and diffraction occur together. A classic example is Young's double-slit experiment. 1. Diffraction: As light passes through each of the two narrow slits, it undergoes diffraction, causing the light to spread out from each slit. 2. Interference: The diffracted waves from the two slits then overlap and interfere with each other, creating an interference pattern. The resulting pattern on the screen is an interference pattern (due to the two slits) whose intensity is modulated by the diffraction pattern of a single slit. This means the overall envelope of the interference fringes is determined by the single-slit diffraction pattern, causing the intensity of the interference fringes to decrease as you move away from the center.

B. NUMERICAL QUESTIONS (5 marks each)

Q6.3: Young's Double Slit - Fringe Width [5 marks]

In Young's double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 600 nm is used. Two slits are separated by 0.2 mm. The screen is 1 m away. Find: (a) Fringe width (b) Distance of 3rd bright fringe from center (c) Distance of 4th dark fringe from center (d) Intensity at a point where path difference is λ/4

* Concept: Interference fringes form due to path difference. Bright fringes (constructive) occur at integer multiples of wavelength (mλ), and Dark fringes (destructive) occur at half-integer multiples of wavelength ((m + ½)λ). Fringe width is the distance between two consecutive bright or dark fringes.

Formula: * Fringe Width: β = λD/d * Position of n-th Bright Fringe: yn_bright = nβ * Position of n-th Dark Fringe: yn_dark = (n - 0.5)β * Intensity at a point: I = Imax cos²(δ/2) where δ = (2π/λ)Δx (Δx is path difference)

Calculation: Given: λ = 600 nm = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m = 6 × 10⁻⁷ m d = 0.2 mm = 0.2 × 10⁻³ m = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m D = 1 m

(a) Fringe Width (β): β = (λD) / d = (6 × 10⁻⁷ m × 1 m) / (2 × 10⁻⁴ m) β = 3 × 10⁻³ m = 3 mm

(b) Distance of 3rd Bright Fringe from center (y₃_bright): For bright fringes, n = 3. y₃_bright = 3β = 3 × (3 mm) = 9 mm

(c) Distance of 4th Dark Fringe from center (y₄_dark): For dark fringes, n = 4. y₄_dark = (4 - 0.5)β = 3.5β = 3.5 × (3 mm) = 10.5 mm

(d) Intensity at a point where path difference Δx = λ/4: First, calculate the phase difference (δ): δ = (2π/λ)Δx = (2π/λ)(λ/4) = π/2 radians

Assuming Imax = 4I₀ (where I₀ is intensity from a single slit): I = Imax cos²(δ/2) = 4I₀ cos²((π/2)/2) = 4I₀ cos²(π/4) I = 4I₀ (1/√2)² = 4I₀ (1/2) = 2I₀

Q6.4: Single Slit Diffraction - Minima [5 marks]

Monochromatic light of wavelength 500 nm passes through a single slit of width 2 × 10⁻⁵ m. Find: (a) Angle of the first diffraction minimum (b) Angle of the second diffraction minimum (c) Width of central maximum on a screen 1 m away (d) Intensity at angle 30° from central maximum

* Actually, for single slit: I(θ) = I₀ [sin(πasinθ/λ) / (πasinθ/λ)]²

At θ = 30°: πasinθ/λ = π × (2 × 10⁻⁵) × 0.5 / (5 × 10⁻⁷) = 10π ≈ 31.4

I(30°) = I₀ [sin(31.4) / 31.4]² sin(31.4) ≈ -0.316 (oscillating function) I(30°) = I₀ × (-0.316/31.4)² = I₀ × (0.001)² ≈ 10⁻⁶ I₀

The intensity is extremely small at 30° (far into diffraction pattern).
