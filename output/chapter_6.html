A. THEORETICAL QUESTIONS (3 marks each)

Q6.1: Interference Condition [3 marks]

What are coherent sources? State the conditions for constructive and destructive interference. Why are ordinary light sources incoherent?

* Coherent Sources: Sources are coherent if they maintain a constant phase difference and have the same frequency. Waves from coherent sources can produce stable interference patterns.

Conditions for Interference:

Constructive Interference (Bright Fringes):

• Path difference: Δ = mλ (where m = 0, 1, 2, 3...)
• Resultant intensity: Imax = (√I₁ + √I₂)²
• For equal intensities I₁ = I₂ = I: Imax = 4I (intensity quadruples!)

Destructive Interference (Dark Fringes):

• Path difference: Δ = (m + ½)λ (where m = 0, 1, 2...)
• Resultant intensity: Imin = (√I₁ - √I₂)²
• For equal intensities: Imin = 0 (complete cancellation possible!)

Why Ordinary Light is Incoherent:

1. Ordinary Sources Emit Randomly:

• Atoms emit light independently
• Each atom emits for ~10⁻⁸ seconds (coherence time)
• After this, the phase changes randomly

2. No Fixed Phase Relationship:

• Multiple atoms don't maintain constant phase difference
• Phase difference changes randomly and rapidly
• Average phase difference is random

3. Incoherent Light Characteristics:

• Cannot produce stable interference patterns with another incoherent source
• If you try double-slit with two separate lamps, no interference pattern forms
• Pattern keeps changing randomly due to phase fluctuations

4. Why Laser Light is Different:

• Stimulated emission produces coherent waves
• All waves have same phase and frequency
• Constant phase relationship maintained
• Produces clear, stable interference patterns

Modern Coherent Sources:

• Lasers (most coherent)
• LED with filters
• Single slit (coherent portions of ordinary light)

Q6.2: Diffraction vs Interference [3 marks]

Distinguish between interference and diffraction. Why does diffraction occur? Can an object have both interference and diffraction simultaneously?

* Interference:

• Superposition of waves from two (or more) separate coherent sources
• Waves combine: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)cosδ
• Results in regular pattern of bright and dark bands
• Requires coherence between sources

Diffraction:

• Bending of waves around obstacles or through apertures
• Wave from single source is modified by an obstacle/aperture
• Results in spreading of waves
• Doesn't require coherence (occurs even with single source)

Key Differences:

| Feature | Interference | Diffraction | |---------|--------------|------------| | Number of sources | Two or more coherent sources | Single source, one wavefront | | Requirement | Coherence needed | No coherence needed | | Pattern | Multiple sharp fringes | Central max + side lobes | | Order of pattern | Uniform spacing | Non-uniform intensity | | Obstruction | Not essential | Essential (slit/obstacle) |

Why Diffraction Occurs:

According to Huygens' Principle, each point on a wavefront becomes a secondary source. When a wavefront passes through a slit or around an obstacle:

• Parts of the wavefront are blocked
• Remaining parts diffract (spread) into the shadow region
• Wavelets spread beyond the geometric shadow

Can Both Occur Together?

YES! This is actually common:

Example: Double-Slit Interference Pattern 1. Light from single source passes through two slits 2. Diffraction: Each slit acts as a secondary source. Light diffracts through each slit 3. Interference: Diffracted light from slit 1 interferes with diffracted light from slit 2 4. Result: Fringes, BUT with varying intensity!

Why Intensity Varies:

• Central region: Both slits contribute (interference fringes bright)
• Sides: Diffraction from individual slits reduces (fringes dim)
• Far sides: Diffraction maxima of one slit aligns with diffraction minima of other slit

The Combined Pattern:

• Interference sets fringe spacing (λD/d)
• Diffraction sets overall intensity envelope
• Central bright fringes are bright
• Higher order fringes fade (diffraction dimming)

B. NUMERICAL QUESTIONS (5 marks each)

Q6.3: Young's Double Slit - Fringe Width [5 marks]

In Young's double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 600 nm is used. Two slits are separated by 0.2 mm. The screen is 1 m away. Find: (a) Fringe width (b) Distance of 3rd bright fringe from center (c) Distance of 4th dark fringe from center (d) Intensity at a point where path difference is λ/4

*

Given: λ = 600 nm = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m = 6 × 10⁻⁷ m, d = 0.2 mm = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m, D = 1 m

(a) Fringe Width (β): β = λD/d = (6 × 10⁻⁷ × 1) / (2 × 10⁻⁴) β = 6 × 10⁻⁷ / (2 × 10⁻⁴) = 3 × 10⁻³ m β = 3 mm

(b) Distance of 3rd Bright Fringe: For bright fringe: ym = mβ = mλD/d y₃ = 3β = 3 × 3 = 9 mm

Or: Using path difference = 3λ: y = 3λD/d = 3 × (6 × 10⁻⁷ × 1) / (2 × 10⁻⁴) = 9 × 10⁻³ m ✓

(c) Distance of 4th Dark Fringe: For dark fringe: ym = (m - ½)β = (m - ½)λD/d y₄ = (4 - 0.5)β = 3.5 × 3 = 10.5 mm

Or: Path difference = (4 - ½)λ = 3.5λ ✓

(d) Intensity at Path Difference = λ/4:

Path difference: Δ = λ/4 Phase difference: δ = (2π/λ) × Δ = (2π/λ) × (λ/4) = π/2 radians = 90°

Resultant intensity: I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂)cosδ

Assuming equal intensities I₁ = I₂ = I₀: I = I₀ + I₀ + 2I₀ cos(π/2) I = 2I₀ + 2I₀ × 0 = 2I₀

Where I₀ is intensity from one slit.

Intensity is 2I₀, which is intermediate between minimum (0) and maximum (4I₀).

Q6.4: Single Slit Diffraction - Minima [5 marks]

Monochromatic light of wavelength 500 nm passes through a single slit of width 2 × 10⁻⁵ m. Find: (a) Angle of the first diffraction minimum (b) Angle of the second diffraction minimum (c) Width of central maximum on a screen 1 m away (d) Intensity at angle 30° from central maximum

*

Given: λ = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5 × 10⁻⁷ m, a = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m, D = 1 m

(a) First Diffraction Minimum: Condition: a sinθ = mλ (m = 1, 2, 3...) For first minimum (m = 1): sinθ₁ = λ/a = (5 × 10⁻⁷) / (2 × 10⁻⁵) sinθ₁ = 0.025 θ₁ = 1.43° (or 0.025 radians)

(b) Second Diffraction Minimum: For m = 2: sinθ₂ = 2λ/a = 2 × (5 × 10⁻⁷) / (2 × 10⁻⁵) sinθ₂ = 0.05 θ₂ = 2.87° (or 0.05 radians)

(c) Width of Central Maximum: The central maximum extends from -θ₁ to +θ₁ Width = 2y₁ = 2 × D × tanθ₁ Width ≈ 2 × D × sinθ₁ (for small angles) Width = 2 × 1 × 0.025 = 0.05 m = 5 cm

Or: Width = 2λD/a = (2 × 5 × 10⁻⁷ × 1) / (2 × 10⁻⁵) = 0.05 m ✓

(d) Intensity at θ = 30°:

At θ = 30°, path difference from top and bottom of slit: β = a sinθ = 2 × 10⁻⁵ × sin(30°) = 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.5 = 10⁻⁵ m = 0.01 mm

Phase difference: δ = (2π/λ) × (β/2) = (2π/(5×10⁻⁷)) × (10⁻⁵/2) δ = (2π × 10⁻⁵) / (2 × 5 × 10⁻⁷) = πl × 10 = 10π radians

Actually, for single slit: I(θ) = I₀ [sin(πasinθ/λ) / (πasinθ/λ)]²

At θ = 30°: πasinθ/λ = π × (2 × 10⁻⁵) × 0.5 / (5 × 10⁻⁷) = 10π ≈ 31.4

I(30°) = I₀ [sin(31.4) / 31.4]² sin(31.4) ≈ -0.316 (oscillating function) I(30°) = I₀ × (-0.316/31.4)² = I₀ × (0.001)² ≈ 10⁻⁶ I₀

The intensity is extremely small at 30° (far into diffraction pattern).
