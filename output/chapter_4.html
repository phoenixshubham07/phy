Q4.1: Moment of Inertia [3 marks]

Define moment of inertia and explain how it's different from mass. Derive the moment of inertia of a uniform rod about one end.

* Moment of Inertia (I) Definition: I = Σ mᵢrᵢ² (for discrete masses) I = ∫ r² dm (for continuous distribution)

It represents the rotational inertia—the resistance of an object to change in rotational motion. Units: kg·m²

Difference from Mass:

• Mass (m): Resistance to linear acceleration (F = ma)
• Moment of Inertia (I): Resistance to angular acceleration (τ = Iα)
• Both are properties of resistance, but to different types of motion
• I depends on mass distribution (axis dependent), m doesn't

Derivation for Uniform Rod About One End:

Consider a uniform rod of length L and mass M. Take the end as origin, x-axis along rod.

Mass element: dm = (M/L) dx (uniform density)

Distance from axis: r = x

Moment of Inertia: I = ∫ r² dm = ∫₀ᴸ x² (M/L) dx

I = (M/L) ∫₀ᴸ x² dx

I = (M/L) [x³/3]₀ᴸ

I = (M/L) × (L³/3)

I = (1/3) ML²

Comparison with Other Axes:

• About center: Icenter = (1/12) ML²
• About one end: Iend = (1/3) ML² = 4 × Icenter

Why? At the end, most mass is farther from axis, hence larger I.

Q4.2: Rolling Motion on Incline [3 marks]

Explain rolling without slipping. Show that a rolling object down an incline has different velocity than a sliding object on a frictionless incline.

* Rolling Without Slipping Condition: At any instant, the point of contact with the ground has zero velocity.

This creates the constraint: v = ωr where v = linear velocity of center, ω = angular velocity, r = radius

Total Kinetic Energy in Rolling: KEtotal = KEtranslational + KErotational KEtotal = ½mv² + ½Iω²

Using ω = v/r and I = kmr² (k depends on shape): KEtotal = ½mv² + ½(kmr²)(v/r)² KEtotal = ½mv² + ½kmv² KEtotal = ½mv²(1 + k)

Rolling vs Sliding Down Incline:

For Rolling Sphere (I = ⅖mr²), k = ⅖:

Using energy conservation from height h: mgh = ½mv² + ½(⅖mr²)(v/r)² mgh = ½mv² + ⅕mv² mgh = (7/10)mv² vrolling = √(10gh/7)

For Sliding (frictionless): No rotational energy mgh = ½mv² vsliding = √(2gh)

Comparison: vrolling / vsliding = √(10gh/7) / √(2gh) = √(10/(7×2)) = √(5/7) ≈ 0.845

Rolling sphere is 15.5% slower than sliding (for same height)

Physical Reason: Some gravitational potential energy goes into rotational motion, not just translation. Therefore, translational velocity is lower.

Q4.3: Geostationary Satellites [3 marks]

What is a geostationary satellite? Explain the three conditions required for a satellite to be geostationary. Why is the orbital radius always 42,000 km?

* Definition: A geostationary satellite appears stationary relative to Earth—it remains over the same point on the equator throughout its orbit.

Three Essential Conditions:

1) Orbital Period = Earth's Rotation Period

• T = 24 hours = 86,400 seconds
• If T ≠ 24 hours, satellite will drift relative to Earth's surface

2) Orbit Above Equator

• The orbital plane must coincide with Earth's equatorial plane
• Any other orbital plane would result in the satellite moving North-South relative to ground

3) Same Direction as Earth's Rotation

• West-to-East (counterclockwise from North Pole view)
• Must revolve in same direction as Earth, not against it

Why Orbital Radius is Always 42,000 km:

For circular orbit, gravitational force provides centripetal force: GMm/r² = mω²r = m(2π/T)²r

GM/r² = (4π²/T²)r

r³ = (GMT²)/(4π²)

r = ∛[GMT²/(4π²)]

Substituting values:

• G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²
• M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg (Earth's mass)
• T = 86,400 s

r³ = (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × 86400²) / (4π²) r³ ≈ 7.496 × 10²² m³ r ≈ 4.22 × 10⁷ m = 42,200 km (from Earth's center)

Height above surface: h = r - REarth = 42,200 - 6,371 = 35,829 km ≈ 36,000 km

This orbital radius is UNIQUE and FIXED because:

• Increase T → need larger r (farther away)
• Decrease T → need smaller r (closer)
• Only one r satisfies T = 24 hours

Applications of Geostationary Satellites:

• Telecommunications
• Weather monitoring
• TV broadcasting
• Must always stay over same spot

Q4.4: Rolling Sphere on Incline [5 marks]

A uniform solid sphere of radius 0.2 m and mass 2 kg rolls without slipping down an incline of height 5 m. Find: (a) Velocity of center of mass at bottom (b) Angular velocity at bottom (c) Rotational kinetic energy at bottom (d) Translational kinetic energy at bottom

(For solid sphere, I = ⅖mr², g = 10 m/s²)

*

Given: r = 0.2 m, m = 2 kg, h = 5 m, I = ⅖mr² = ⅖(2)(0.2)² = 0.032 kg·m²

Using Energy Conservation: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω²

With ω = v/r: mgh = ½mv² + ½(⅖mr²)(v/r)² mgh = ½mv² + ⅕mv² mgh = (7/10)mv²

(a) Velocity at Bottom: v² = (10gh)/7 = (10 × 10 × 5)/7 = 500/7 = 71.43 v = 8.45 m/s

(b) Angular Velocity at Bottom: ω = v/r = 8.45/0.2 = 42.25 rad/s

(c) Rotational Kinetic Energy: KErot = ½Iω² = ½(0.032)(42.25)² = ½(0.032)(1785) = 28.56 Joules

(d) Translational Kinetic Energy: KEtrans = ½mv² = ½(2)(8.45)² = (1)(71.4) = 71.4 Joules

Verification: KEtotal = 28.56 + 71.4 = 99.96 ≈ 100 J mgh = 2 × 10 × 5 = 100 J ✓

Ratio Check: KErot / KEtrans = (⅕mv²) / (½mv²) = (2/5) / (1/2) = 4/5 = 0.8 28.56 / 71.4 ≈ 0.4 (which is 2/5 of translational) ✓

Q4.5: Geostationary Satellite Orbital Velocity [5 marks]

Calculate the orbital velocity of a geostationary satellite. Use:

• Earth's mass: M = 6 × 10²⁴ kg
• G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²
• Orbital radius: r = 4.2 × 10⁷ m

Also find the velocity change needed if the satellite moves to a polar orbit at radius 6.6 × 10⁶ m.

*

(a) Geostationary Orbital Velocity:

For circular orbit: v = √(GM/r)

vgeo = √(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ / (4.2 × 10⁷))

vgeo = √(40.02 × 10¹³ / (4.2 × 10⁷))

vgeo = √(9.53 × 10⁶)

vgeo = 3,087 m/s = 3.09 km/s

Verification using T = 24 hours: v = 2πr/T = (2π × 4.2 × 10⁷) / 86,400 = 2.64 × 10⁸ / 86,400 = 3,056 m/s ✓ (Close match, slight difference due to rounding)

(b) Polar Orbit at Lower Altitude:

rpolar = 6.6 × 10⁶ m

vpolar = √(GM/rpolar)

vpolar = √(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6 × 10²⁴ / (6.6 × 10⁶))

vpolar = √(40.02 × 10¹³ / (6.6 × 10⁶))

vpolar = √(6.06 × 10⁶)

vpolar = 2,462 m/s = 2.46 km/s

(c) Change in Velocity: Δv = vgeo - vpolar = 3,087 - 2,462 = 625 m/s

This is the reduction in orbital velocity when moving to lower orbit (higher speed needed to be closer to Earth for same period).

Note: Actually, for a polar orbit to be stable, we need different equations, but this shows the basic orbital mechanics principle: closer orbits require lower velocities.

Q4.6: Moment of Inertia Calculation [5 marks]

A uniform disk of radius R = 0.3 m and mass M = 5 kg is rotated about its center. A point mass m = 1 kg is placed at distance r = 0.2 m from the axis. Find: (a) Moment of inertia of disk alone (b) Moment of inertia of point mass (c) Total moment of inertia (d) Angular acceleration if a torque of 2 N·m is applied

*

(a) Moment of Inertia of Uniform Disk: For disk about center: Idisk = ½MR² = ½(5)(0.3)² = ½(5)(0.09) = 0.225 kg·m²

(b) Moment of Inertia of Point Mass: Imass = mr² = 1 × (0.2)² = 0.04 kg·m²

(c) Total Moment of Inertia: Itotal = Idisk + Imass = 0.225 + 0.04 = 0.265 kg·m²

(d) Angular Acceleration: Using τ = Iα: α = τ / Itotal = 2 / 0.265 = 7.55 rad/s²

Physical Interpretation:

• The disk contributes 85% of total rotational inertia (0.225/0.265)
• The point mass contributes only 15%
• Even though the point mass is small, its effect on angular acceleration is significant
• The system resists angular acceleration with I = 0.265 kg·m²

Q4.7: Center of Mass [3 marks]

Define Centre of Mass (CM). Give the formula for the position of the centre of mass of a two-body system.

* Definition: The centre of mass is the point where the entire mass of the system can be considered concentrated for analyzing its translational motion. It represents the weighted average position of the mass distribution.

Formula for Two-Body System: For two masses m₁ and m₂ at positions r₁ and r₂: Rcm = (m₁r₁ + m₂r₂) / (m₁ + m₂)

Significance:

• If m₁ >> m₂, the CM is closer to m₁.
• External forces act as if applied at the CM.
